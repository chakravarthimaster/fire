# What's Worth Building: The Written Guide

*A companion to the film. It covers how to build a resilient, top-tier career in
product management while AI keeps getting more capable, possibly all the way to
AGI and beyond.*

The film gives the shape of the answer in nine minutes. This guide gives the
detail: what to learn, in what order, how to practise it, and how to prove it
to an employer.

## 1. The short answer

**Is product management a good career for someone starting today?** Yes, but
not the old version of the job.

- **AI is absorbing the production work.** That means first drafts of specs,
  research summaries, data pulls, meeting notes, status reports, tickets,
  competitive scans and even working prototypes.
- **The judgment work is growing.** That means choosing which problems matter,
  understanding customers deeply, deciding between good options, defining what
  "good" means for an AI system, aligning people, and being accountable for
  outcomes and for trust.
- **When building gets cheap, deciding what to build gets expensive.** The
  scarcest skill in an AI-rich world is knowing what is *worth* building.

The "super level", the product manager who stays valuable no matter how capable
the tools become, can be summed up as:

> **Super PM = seven layers of skill, crowned by judgment × learning speed.**

| # | Layer | One-line summary |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Customer obsession | See what people need, including what they don't say. |
| 2 | Product sense and taste | Pick the right answer out of a hundred AI-generated options. |
| 3 | AI fluency | Understand models, their limits, their evaluations and their cost. |
| 4 | Builder skills | Prototype, query and ship yourself. Show, don't just tell. |
| 5 | Data and experimentation | Choose the metric that matters and read results honestly. |
| 6 | Domain depth | Deep knowledge of one industry, combined with AI. |
| 7 | Leadership, ethics and trust | Align people, communicate clearly, catch risks before they ship. |
| 👑 | **Judgment × learning speed** | Decide well under uncertainty, and keep learning as tools change every few months. |

**The path** has four stages: Foundations (months 0–3), Build (months 3–6),
Specialize (months 6–12) and Lead (years 1–3). Seven habits keep you
resilient along the way, and there is more than one door into the profession.

## 2. Three horizons: AI, AGI, ASI

Nobody knows the timeline. Some people expect human-level AI within years,
others within decades, and some doubt it will arrive at all. So the smart move
is not to predict the future but to **build a career that works in all three
scenarios**.

| Horizon | What it is | What happens to product work | What becomes more valuable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AI (now)** | Tools that write, analyze, design and code alongside people. | Production work gets faster and cheaper. One PM can prototype an idea in an afternoon, test it with users that evening and have data by morning. | Problem selection, customer insight, taste, speed of learning, and the ability to build with AI yourself. |
| **AGI (possible)** | Systems that could match people at most mental work, and act on their own. | AI agents research, design, code, test and ship. The PM becomes an **orchestrator of intelligence**: setting goals, constraints and the definition of success, then judging the results. You manage outcomes, not a backlog. | Clear goals, well-specified constraints, evaluation design, guardrails, and accountability for what agents do. |
| **ASI (speculative)** | Systems that would surpass people at nearly everything. | The question shifts from *how* to build to *what should exist*. Whose problems come first? Which trade-offs are acceptable? What should never be built? | Values, trust, responsibility and governance. These questions belong to people. |

**What works in every scenario:** understanding people, judging well, building
fast, and earning trust. Everything in this guide is chosen because it pays off
now *and* keeps paying off if the more dramatic horizons arrive.

## 3. The verdict: is product management still a good career?

### What is at risk

The product manager whose week is mostly **production and coordination**:

- writing long documents nobody reads
- running status meetings and producing status reports
- grooming backlogs and writing tickets
- relaying information between teams

AI does much of this well already. Entry-level roles built mainly on this work
are becoming harder to find, and the hiring bar for junior product managers is
rising. That is the honest part of the answer.

### What is rising

The product manager who:

- **understands customers deeply** because they talk to them constantly
- **builds with AI** and prototypes ideas instead of only describing them
- **makes sound decisions under uncertainty** and can explain them in writing
- **has more leverage than ever**, because one person with AI tools can now
  do what used to take a small team

That product manager is more valuable than ever. The question isn't whether AI
will take the job. It's whether you'll grow into the new one.

### Is it a good fit for you?

It is likely a strong fit if you:

- enjoy ambiguity and being responsible for "why" and "what", not only "how"
- like talking to people, including those who disagree with you
- get excited about building things, and are willing to learn technical skills
- can make a call, own the outcome, and change your mind when evidence says so

It may be a weaker fit if you want a clearly defined, purely individual craft
with little negotiation, or if you dislike being accountable for outcomes you
don't fully control.

### The trade-off, stated plainly

The bar to enter is higher and the path in is less standardized than it was.
But the ceiling is higher too. A product manager who can talk to users in the
morning, prototype with AI at lunch and read the data by evening learns faster
than any product manager in history.

## 4. The Super PM: seven layers of skill, and the crown

Each layer rests on the one below. For each one you'll find why it matters now,
what "super" looks like, how to practise it, and what to put in your
portfolio as proof.

### Layer 1: Customer obsession

**Why it matters now:** AI can summarize what people said. Only you can sit
with them and notice what they *didn't* say: the workaround, the hesitation, the
spreadsheet they're embarrassed about.

**What "super" looks like**
- Talks to users every week, not once a quarter.
- Knows customers' goals, frustrations and workarounds, and can tell stories
  about specific people.
- Separates what people *say* from what they *do*.
- Turns raw conversations into sharp problem statements.

**How to practise**
- Run one customer conversation per week. Ask about the past ("tell me about
  the last time…"), not hypotheticals ("would you use…?").
- Map each problem with the jobs-to-be-done lens: *when [situation], I want to
  [motivation], so I can [outcome].*
- Use AI to transcribe and cluster interviews, then check its clusters against
  your own notes. The disagreements are where insight hides.

**Proof for your portfolio:** an interview synthesis showing a non-obvious
insight and the decision it changed.

### Layer 2: Product sense and taste

**Why it matters now:** when AI can generate a hundred designs, specs or
strategies in a minute, the valuable skill is choosing the right one and
explaining why.

**What "super" looks like**
- Can explain why a product works, or doesn't, in terms of user psychology,
  friction, trust and value.
- Recognizes quality quickly and articulates it precisely.
- Knows when "good enough" is right and when craft matters.

**How to practise**
- Do a weekly teardown of one great product and one broken one. Write down
  the job the product does, the key decisions behind it and what you'd change.
- Ship small things and invite critique. Taste is trained through exposure,
  critique and shipping.
- Keep a swipe file of great onboarding flows, empty states, pricing pages and
  AI interactions.

**Proof for your portfolio:** a public series of short product teardowns with
concrete, reasoned recommendations.

### Layer 3: AI fluency

**Why it matters now:** you can't lead what you don't understand. AI features
fail in new ways: they are probabilistic, they can be confidently wrong, and
every call costs money and time.

**What "super" looks like**
- Understands how models work at a practical level: tokens, context windows,
  hallucinations, retrieval (RAG), fine-tuning versus prompting, agents and
  tool use.
- **Writes evaluations** ("evals"): test sets and scoring rules that define
  what good behavior looks like before launch and catch regressions after.
- Knows the **cost and speed** of every AI feature: tokens per request, latency
  users will tolerate, when to use a smaller model, caching, and fallbacks.
- Designs for failure: what happens when the model is wrong, slow or
  unavailable? Where does a human stay in the loop?

**How to practise**
- Use AI tools daily for real work, and notice where they fail.
- Take one AI feature (yours or a public one) and write 30–50 test cases, a
  scoring rubric and a pass bar. That is an eval set.
- Compare two models on the same eval and write a one-page recommendation
  covering quality, latency and cost.

**Proof for your portfolio:** an eval report for a real AI feature, including
failure analysis and a cost estimate.

### Layer 4: Builder skills

**Why it matters now:** the best product managers of this era don't just tell.
They show. A working prototype ends debates that documents can't.

**What "super" looks like**
- Prototypes their own ideas with AI coding tools and gets them in front of
  users.
- Knows enough **SQL** and **Python** to answer their own questions without
  waiting on a data team.
- Knows enough about **APIs** to know what's possible, what's expensive and
  what's hard.
- Respects engineering. Prototypes are for learning, not for bypassing the
  team.

**How to practise**
- Build one small tool a month with an AI coding assistant, and deploy it
  somewhere real.
- Answer one product question a week with your own SQL query.
- Read the API docs of one AI provider and build a tiny app that calls it.

**Proof for your portfolio:** two or three small products that real people
used, with links, screenshots and what you learned.

### Layer 5: Data and experimentation

**Why it matters now:** AI makes it cheap to generate ideas and run tests, so
the constraint becomes choosing the right metric and reading results honestly,
especially when the data disagrees with you.

**What "super" looks like**
- Chooses the metric that truly matters: a north-star metric, the input metrics
  that drive it, and guardrail metrics that must not get worse.
- Designs clean experiments: a clear hypothesis, enough users to detect the
  effect, a fixed test duration, and no peeking.
- Knows the traps: novelty effects, sample-ratio mismatch, Simpson's paradox,
  and confusing correlation with causation.
- Measures AI quality, not just engagement: task success, human ratings,
  error rates, and the cost per successful outcome.

**How to practise**
- Draw a metric tree for a product you use.
- Re-analyze a public A/B test write-up and critique its conclusions.
- For your own projects, write down your prediction *before* you look at the
  data.

**Proof for your portfolio:** a metric tree and an experiment write-up with
the hypothesis, design, result and decision, including one where you were wrong.

### Layer 6: Domain depth

**Why it matters now:** general AI knowledge is becoming common. Deep knowledge
of an industry, combined with AI, is rare, and rare is valuable.

**What "super" looks like**
- Knows one domain (for example health, finance, education, climate,
  logistics, security or legal) well enough to talk to experts as a peer.
- Understands its data, its workflows, its economics and its rules.
- Sees where AI changes the domain, and where regulation or trust makes that
  hard.

**How to practise**
- Pick a domain you're curious about or already close to.
- Read its trade publications. Learn its jargon and its regulations. Interview
  ten practitioners about their workflows.
- Build your Stage 2 and Stage 3 projects in that domain.

**Proof for your portfolio:** a domain landscape write-up plus one AI product
built for that domain's real workflow.

### Layer 7: Leadership, ethics and trust

**Why it matters now:** as machines do more of the work, the people we trust
to decide become priceless.

**What "super" looks like**
- **Aligns people who want different things.** Makes trade-offs explicit and
  gets to a decision.
- **Communicates with clarity.** Short memos, clear decisions, good stories.
- **Catches risks before they ship:** privacy, security (including prompt
  injection and data leakage), fairness, safety and transparency.
- Knows the rules taking shape around AI, such as the European Union's AI Act,
  and the norms of their own industry.

**How to practise**
- Write a one-page decision memo for every significant decision: context,
  options, the choice and why, and what would change your mind.
- Run a pre-mortem before each launch: "It's six months from now and this
  failed. Why?"
- For every AI feature, write a short risk review covering who could be
  harmed, how, how you'd detect it, and what the fallback is.

**Proof for your portfolio:** decision memos and one responsible-AI review of a
feature you built.

### The crown: judgment × learning speed

This is the skill that ties everything together.

**Judgment** means deciding well under uncertainty:
- Separate reversible decisions (move fast) from irreversible ones (slow down).
- Estimate expected value and opportunity cost, since every "yes" is a "no" to
  something else.
- Use pre-mortems and a **decision journal** that records what you decided,
  why, and what you expected, then review it later. This is how judgment
  improves.
- Be calibrated. Say how confident you are, and track whether you were right.

**Learning speed** means how fast you learn, unlearn and relearn:
- Tools will change every few months, so your ability to learn is the moat that
  lasts.
- Keep a weekly "new tool, real task" experiment.
- Retire old habits deliberately: "What did I do last year that AI now does
  better?"

### Self-assessment

Rate yourself from 1 to 5 on each layer every quarter. Then pick **one** layer
to raise by one point next quarter.

| Layer | 1: Beginner | 3: Solid | 5: Super |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer obsession | Has done a few interviews | Talks to users monthly and synthesizes well | Weekly conversations; insights regularly change the roadmap |
| Product sense and taste | Has opinions | Explains why products work | Consistently picks winners and articulates why |
| AI fluency | Uses chat assistants | Understands RAG, agents and evals; has written an eval set | Designs AI systems with evals, cost models and failure handling |
| Builder skills | No code | Prototypes with AI tools; basic SQL | Ships real tools; answers own data questions; fluent with APIs |
| Data and experimentation | Reads dashboards | Designs simple A/B tests; builds metric trees | Designs rigorous experiments; honest about null results |
| Domain depth | Curious | Conversant with experts | Respected by domain experts; sees AI opportunities first |
| Leadership, ethics, trust | Follows | Aligns a team; writes clear memos | Aligns an organization; the person others trust with hard calls |
| **Judgment × learning speed** | Decides by instinct | Uses frameworks; reviews decisions | Calibrated, fast and humble; learning compounds visibly |

## 5. The knowledge map

Build knowledge in three rings. You don't need to be an expert in all of it;
aim for "enough to make good decisions and ask sharp questions".

### Ring 1: The product core

| Topic | Know enough to… |
|---|---|
| Discovery | Run interviews, find root problems, and test assumptions cheaply. |
| Strategy | Explain where you'll play, how you'll win and what you won't do. |
| Prioritization | Rank opportunities by value, confidence and cost, and say no clearly. |
| Pricing and unit economics | Understand willingness to pay, margins, customer acquisition cost versus lifetime value, and AI inference cost per user. |
| Go-to-market | Position a product, pick a channel and plan a launch. |
| User psychology | Reason about motivation, friction, habits, trust and cognitive load. |

### Ring 2: The AI core

| Topic | Know enough to… |
|---|---|
| Language models | Explain tokens, context windows, sampling, and why models hallucinate. |
| Retrieval (RAG) | Decide when to ground a model in your own data, and how to measure retrieval quality. |
| Fine-tuning | Know when fine-tuning beats prompting or retrieval, and when it doesn't. |
| Agents and tools | Design multi-step AI workflows with tools, permissions, checkpoints and human review. |
| Evaluation | Write eval sets, choose metrics and rubrics, and know the limits of using a model as the judge. |
| Economics of running models | Estimate cost per request, choose between models, and use caching, batching and routing. |

### Ring 3: The responsibility core

| Topic | Know enough to… |
|---|---|
| Privacy | Minimize data collection, handle consent, and know what not to send to a model. |
| Security | Recognize prompt injection, data leakage and over-permissioned agents. |
| Fairness | Test for uneven performance across user groups. |
| Safety | Design guardrails, escalation paths and kill switches. |
| Regulation | Know the main AI rules that apply to your market (for example the EU AI Act, which phases in obligations by risk level) and your sector's own rules. |

## 6. The roadmap: four stages

The timeline assumes roughly **8–10 focused hours a week** alongside a job or
studies. Move faster if you can. Don't skip the building.

### Stage 1: Foundations (months 0–3)

**Goal:** understand the craft and the technology, and start using AI every day.

- **Read the classics of product management.** Start with one each on
  discovery, strategy and execution (see the [reading list](#10-reading-and-practice-list)).
- **Learn how AI models work.** Watch one good introductory lecture series and
  build a tiny app that calls a model through an API.
- **Use AI tools every day** for writing, research, analysis and code, and keep
  a log of where they help and where they fail.
- **Take apart ten AI products.** For each, write down the job it does, how it
  probably works (model, retrieval, tools), how it handles failure, and what
  you'd measure.

**Weekly rhythm:** 3 hours reading or courses, 2 hours of AI practice, 2 hours
of teardowns, and 1 hour writing up what you learned.

**Exit criteria:** ten teardowns written, one tiny AI app running, and you can
explain RAG, evals and agents to a friend.

### Stage 2: Build (months 3–6)

**Goal:** prove you can take an idea from problem to shipped product with real
users.

- **Ship two or three small AI products end to end, with real users.** Real
  means even ten people who aren't your friends.
- For each one, **write the spec, define the metrics, design the evaluations**,
  launch, and measure.
- **Publish what you learned**, including what didn't work.

**Project ideas** (build them in a domain you care about):
- A tool that turns a pile of customer reviews or support tickets into a weekly
  insight report, with an eval showing how accurate its themes are.
- A question-answering assistant over a public body of documents (regulations,
  course material, product manuals) using retrieval, with a golden test set.
- A small agent that completes a multi-step task (for example researching a
  company and drafting a brief) with human checkpoints and a cost-per-task
  budget.
- An AI feature added to an existing open-source product, with a before/after
  experiment.

**Exit criteria:** two or three shipped products, each with a one-page case
study: problem, insight, what you built, metrics, eval results, and the
lessons learned.

### Stage 3: Specialize (months 6–12)

**Goal:** become the person who understands both a domain and AI.

- **Choose your domain.** Learn its data, its workflows and its rules.
- **Interview practitioners.** Map where their time goes and where AI could
  help or hurt.
- **Get close to an AI feature at work**, by volunteering to help on one, or
  **build one on your own** for that domain.
- Write a domain-plus-AI point of view: where the opportunities are, what the
  risks are, and what you'd build first.

**Exit criteria:** you can hold your own with domain experts, and you have one
substantial AI product or feature in that domain with measured results.

### Stage 4: Lead (years 1–3)

**Goal:** own outcomes at a larger scale and multiply others.

- **Own an AI product area**, with its metrics, evals, roadmap and risks.
- **Orchestrate people and agents.** Set goals, constraints and definitions of
  success for teams that mix humans and AI.
- **Mentor others.** Teaching sharpens your own judgment.
- **Share your thinking in public** through writing, talks or open-source
  work. It compounds your reputation and your network.

**Exit criteria:** measurable business outcomes you can point to, a team that
trusts your decisions, and a public body of work.

## 7. Seven habits of a resilient product manager

| # | Habit | What it builds | How to do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Use AI every day** | Fluency | Try a new tool or technique on a *real* task each week; log what worked. |
| 2 | **Talk to a customer every week** | Empathy | Put a standing 30-minute slot in your calendar. Never cancel it for internal meetings. |
| 3 | **Ship something every month** | Momentum | A prototype, a feature, an analysis, a post. Small and real beats big and imaginary. |
| 4 | **Put your thinking in writing** | Clarity | One-page memos for decisions; a weekly note of what you learned. |
| 5 | **Study failures, including your own** | Wisdom | Keep a decision journal; do post-mortems without blame; read other companies' incident write-ups. |
| 6 | **Learn in public** | Reputation | Publish teardowns, case studies and lessons. People hire those they've learned from. |
| 7 | **Protect time to think deeply, away from the screen** | Depth | Block two hours a week with no devices: walk, think, write by hand. Judgment needs quiet. |

## 8. How to break in

There is more than one door.

### Door 1: Associate product manager programs

Several large technology companies run structured entry programs for new
product managers, such as Google's APM program and Meta's Rotational Product
Manager program. They are competitive, and their size changes from year to
year, so check current openings.
- **Stand out** with shipped projects, clear writing and a sharp product point
  of view, not just a good GPA.

### Door 2: An internal move

From engineering, design, data, support or sales. This is often the most
reliable door.
- Volunteer for product work nearby: customer research, specs for small
  features, analyzing a launch.
- Ask a product manager to mentor you, and shadow their meetings.
- Take ownership of one small feature end to end, and make the outcome visible.

### Door 3: An early-stage startup

- Small teams need generalists, and titles matter less than impact.
- Look for "founding product", "product engineer" or "founder's associate"
  roles.
- Show you can ship. A portfolio of built products matters more here than
  anywhere.

### Door 4: Build a real product yourself (the most convincing path)

- Build something people use, measure it, and write it up.
- It proves every layer of the stack at once: customer insight, taste, AI
  fluency, building, data, and judgment.
- Let the results speak. "I built X, it reached Y users, and I learned Z" beats
  any certificate.

### Your portfolio

For each project, write a one-page case study:

1. **Problem:** who has it, and how you know.
2. **Insight:** what you discovered that wasn't obvious.
3. **Solution:** what you built and why you chose it over the alternatives.
4. **AI design:** model choice, retrieval or tools, evals, failure handling and cost.
5. **Results:** metrics, what users said, and what surprised you.
6. **Decisions and lessons:** what you'd do differently.

### Your résumé

Lead with outcomes, not duties:
- ✗ "Responsible for writing product requirements."
- ✓ "Built and launched an AI support-triage prototype used by 40 agents; cut
  first-response time 30% in a 4-week test."

### Interview preparation

Expect some mix of these:

| Interview type | What they test | How to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Product sense | Can you find the right problem and design a good solution? | Practise with a structure: user → problem → options → choose → measure. Always make trade-offs explicit. |
| AI product design | Can you design AI features responsibly? | Cover model approach, data, evals, failure modes, human-in-the-loop, latency, cost and safety. |
| Metrics and execution | Can you define success and diagnose problems? | Practise metric trees, "metric X dropped 10%, why?" cases, and launch plans. |
| Technical depth | Can you work well with engineers? | Explain how an AI feature works end to end, and the cost and latency trade-offs involved. |
| Behavioral and leadership | Can you lead without authority? | Prepare stories about conflict, failure, influence and hard decisions, using situation → action → result. |

## 9. Staying resilient: a quarterly self-check

Every quarter, answer honestly:

1. What share of my time went to **production** (docs, status, coordination)
   versus **judgment** (customers, decisions, building, evaluation)? Push the
   second above 60%.
2. Which of my regular tasks could AI now do? Have I handed them over?
3. How many customers did I talk to? What did I learn that surprised me?
4. What did I ship? What did I learn from it?
5. Which of the seven layers did I raise? Which is my weakest?
6. What did I learn that I didn't know three months ago, and what did I
   unlearn?
7. Would the people I work with trust me with a hard, irreversible decision?

**Warning signs** that you're drifting toward the old job:
- Most of your week is meetings and documents.
- You haven't talked to a customer in a month.
- You can't explain how your product's AI features work or how they're
  evaluated.
- You haven't built anything yourself in a quarter.

- *AI Engineering*, Chip Huyen (building applications on foundation models)
- *Co-Intelligence*, Ethan Mollick (working with AI)
- Andrej Karpathy's introductory talks and videos on large language models
- The prompt engineering, tool use and evaluation guides published by the
  major AI model providers

**Responsibility**
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework
- OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications (security)
- *The Alignment Problem*, Brian Christian
- *Weapons of Math Destruction*, Cathy O'Neil

**Breaking in**
- *Cracking the PM Interview*, Gayle Laakmann McDowell and Jackie Bavaro
- Product management newsletters and podcasts with long-form practitioner
  interviews

**Practice that beats reading:** build, ship, measure and write. One shipped
product teaches more than ten books.

## 11. Glossary: AI terms every product manager should know

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| **Large language model (LLM)** | A model trained on large amounts of text to predict the next token. It can write, summarize, reason and follow instructions. |
| **Token** | A chunk of text (roughly ¾ of a word in English). Models read and write tokens, and pricing is usually per token. |
| **Context window** | How much text a model can consider at once, including your instructions, documents and conversation. |
| **Hallucination** | A confident but false output. It is designed around, not just prompted away. |
| **Prompt** | The instructions and context you give a model. |
| **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** | Looking up relevant documents and giving them to the model so its answer is grounded in your data. |
| **Embedding** | A numeric representation of meaning, used to search for similar text. |
| **Fine-tuning** | Further training a model on your examples to specialize its behavior or style. |
| **Agent** | A system in which a model plans and takes actions (calls tools, browses, writes code) over multiple steps toward a goal. |
| **Tool use** | Letting a model call functions or APIs, such as search, databases or calculators. |
| **Evaluation (eval)** | A test set plus scoring method that measures whether an AI system behaves well. It is the AI equivalent of a test suite and a quality bar. |
| **Guardrails** | Rules and checks that constrain what an AI system can say or do. |
| **Human-in-the-loop** | A design in which people review or approve AI outputs at key points. |
| **Latency** | How long users wait for a response. It shapes the whole experience. |
| **Inference cost** | What it costs to run the model for each request. It directly shapes your unit economics. |
| **Prompt injection** | An attack in which malicious text tricks a model into ignoring its instructions. It is a key security risk for agents. |
| **AGI** | Artificial general intelligence: a hypothetical system that matches people at most mental work. |
